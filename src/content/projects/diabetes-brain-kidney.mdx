## Where this started

People with type 2 diabetes get dementia at roughly twice the rate of people without it.
Not only Alzheimer's — a large share is *vascular* dementia, the kind caused by the slow
accumulation of damage to the brain's smallest blood vessels.

That much is not controversial. What nobody can say is **why**. The obvious answer is high
blood sugar, and the obvious answer appears to be wrong: when you test it genetically,
glucose and HbA1c do essentially nothing to vascular dementia risk, even though diabetes
clearly moves both. Something else about diabetes is doing the damage.

This project spent months trying to find out what.

## The tool

You cannot randomise people to diabetes. But nature has already run something close to a
randomised trial: everyone is dealt a random genetic hand at conception, and some of those
hands nudge people toward higher blood sugar, or more belly fat, or higher blood pressure.
Because the shuffle happens before birth, it is not confounded by diet, income, or exercise
the way an observational study is.

The method that exploits this is called **Mendelian randomisation**. Used carelessly it
produces confident nonsense, so most of the work here is not running the analysis — it is
the negative controls, the instrument-strength diagnostics, and the checks for whether a
result is real or an artifact of the machinery.

## The beautiful idea

Here is the hypothesis this project was built to test.

Fat is not simply harmful. Your body has a *safe place* to store it — the subcutaneous fat
on your hips and thighs, the gluteofemoral depot. That tissue is metabolically calm. What
appears to be dangerous is running out of room there. When the safe depot is full, or small,
or genetically limited, surplus lipid has nowhere good to go. It spills into places it
should not be: the liver, the muscle, the space around the organs, the blood.

This reframes obesity. The question is not *how much* fat you carry; it is **how much you
can safely put away**. On this account, the reason two people with the same BMI have
completely different metabolic fates is that one of them has more storage capacity.

The prediction: limited storage capacity should damage the small vessels — in the brain, in
the kidney — while leaving the heart comparatively alone, because heart disease tracks fat
*quantity*.

## The parts that held up

**Storage capacity is a real, measurable thing, and it is not just BMI in disguise.** When
you put fat *distribution* and total *body mass* into the same model as competing
explanations, they separate cleanly and go to different organs: distribution carries the
small-vessel effects, body mass carries heart failure. Adjusting one for the other
strengthens rather than weakens the result, which is the opposite of what you would see if
it were a statistical artifact.

**The molecular signature is unmistakable.** We measured what 48 different circulating
molecules do in response to good versus failed storage. Ninety-five of those relationships
held under two independent statistical estimators. And the two directions are near-perfect
mirror images: good storage lowers triglycerides, large VLDL particles and branched-chain
amino acids, and **raises** LDL particle diameter. Failed storage does exactly the reverse,
producing the small, dense LDL that is the classic signature of metabolic disease.

That mirror symmetry is the strongest evidence in the project that the construct is real
rather than a statistical shadow. Two instruments built by different groups from different
data, pointing in opposite directions, moving the same molecules oppositely.

**The heart is genuinely spared.** Across every version of the exposure and every
statistical method, cardioembolic stroke and heart failure came back null — while total
body fat hit both hard in the same analyses. Negative controls behaving like negative
controls is what licenses believing the positives.

## The first narrowing: blood pressure

The cerebral effect was real, but it was not *direct*.

Roughly half of the path from storage failure to small-vessel brain disease runs through
blood pressure — about 52% for small-vessel stroke, 45% for lacunar stroke. And when we
built the properly identified model, putting storage capacity, diabetes liability and blood
pressure in together, the storage term **disappeared at the brain** (p=0.13) while blood
pressure remained overwhelming.

This is a mechanism, not a refutation. If poor storage raises blood pressure and blood
pressure injures the brain's penetrating arterioles, that is still a storage-capacity story
— it just has a named conduit, and the conduit is one we already know how to treat. But the
stronger and more interesting claim, that storage failure reaches the brain by some private
route of its own, did not survive.

## The second narrowing: our own broken code

Two errors in our own analysis had been quietly shaping the results.

The first was a **placeholder standard error**. The canonical published instrument for this
hypothesis ships effect sizes but no error estimates, so every model using it had been run
with a stand-in value of 1.0. That is harmless for the effect estimates, but it silently
destroyed the one diagnostic that tells you whether a multi-exposure model is trustworthy at
all. For months, that number had been printing as ~0.0002 — which looks like catastrophic
failure but actually meant *no information*. Rebuilding the same genetic variants from a
different dataset that does report errors made the diagnostic computable for the first time,
and the models that had looked unusable turned out to be fine.

The second was a **flipped effect allele**, and the instructive part is that we made it
twice. The brain-imaging dataset used for white-matter damage reports its effects relative to
the second listed allele, not the first. Read it the natural way and every directional
statement about white matter comes out backwards.

We found this in July, wrote it up, proved it three ways — a positive control at a known
collagen gene, a genetic-correlation sign test, and the server's own documentation — and
filed the conclusion. Seven weeks later a new session wrote fresh extraction code, took the
same natural reading, and reintroduced the identical bug. It survived about six hours and one
batch of reports before three checks caught it again.

The corrected numbers are an exact mirror: one key result read −0.041 before the fix and
+0.041 after, with the p-value, F-statistic and every other quantity identical. P-values
survive a sign flip, so no conclusion about *whether* something mattered changed.

The useful lesson is not "be careful with allele coding." We *were* careful, documented it,
and it still recurred, because prose in a file does not intercept the next person writing a
loader. The fix that actually works is a checked-in reader for that file with an assertion on
a known-positive control — blood pressure must come out as increasing white-matter damage —
so the bug cannot survive a test run. That is now in place.

## The third narrowing: a wall that was not there

One line of work had been formally marked as impossible. The claim on file was that
peripheral insulin sensitivity — how well your muscle and fat actually take up glucose —
could not be studied, because the available data yielded only two usable genetic markers.

That number had been taken from the wrong file. The companion dataset, adjusted for body
mass, yields **eight**, and they are strong. More to the point, look at where they sit:
**PPARG**, the master regulator of fat-cell expansion and the target of an entire class of
diabetes drugs. **GLUT4**, the transporter that actually moves glucose into muscle. **IRS1**
and **COBLL1**, the canonical insulin-signalling and fat-distribution genes.

This is the storage hypothesis written out in genes, assembled without reference to the
variant lists anyone had used before. And when we finally ran it, it gave the cleanest
answer in the entire project — but not the answer we were looking for.

## What it actually found: the kidney

Peripheral insulin resistance causes **albuminuria** — protein leaking into the urine, the
earliest sign of damage to the kidney's filtering vessels. It does so strongly
(p≈10⁻⁴), robustly to every statistical method we tried, and robustly to dropping any single
genetic marker.

And it does **nothing** to the brain. Small-vessel stroke p=0.20. Lacunar stroke p=0.41.
White matter damage p=0.92.

So the organ that limited storage capacity demonstrably reaches is the kidney, not the
brain. The brain effect exists but runs through blood pressure; the kidney effect is direct.
The two organs had been grouped together for most of this project as a single "small vessel"
story. With better tools they come apart.

We then spent a while trying to break that kidney result, because it is now the headline:

- **Is it a measurement artifact?** Urine protein is reported as a *ratio* to creatinine,
  and creatinine tracks muscle mass — and this instrument is built partly from muscle genes.
  If the variants shrink muscle, the ratio rises with no real leak. Tested against a
  450,000-person study of lean mass: no significant effect, and pointing the *opposite*
  way from what the artifact would require.
- **Is it an artifact of studying only diabetics?** Selecting on having diabetes can
  manufacture false associations. Correcting for it, the effect survives — and it is also
  present in the general population, where the correction does not apply at all.

Neither check was in the original analysis. Both came from reading the criticism literature
on this exact method, which is worth doing before rather than after.

## Things that turned out not to be true

Subtraction again did a lot of the work:

- **Storage capacity does not reach vascular dementia.** An earlier result said it did
  (p=0.03). With eight times as many genetic markers it is p=0.18 — a small-sample artifact,
  retracted.
- **It does not affect cognition** — not general intelligence, not reaction time. Total body
  fat does, but that is a different axis.
- **It does not affect white-matter damage** in any version of the exposure.
- **The "brain and kidney share one microvascular vulnerability" framing was premature.** It
  was graded as supported in an earlier write-up. The two organs behave differently under
  every stress test we applied.
- **High blood sugar is not the carrier.** Glucose and HbA1c do essentially nothing to
  vascular dementia even though diabetes moves both substantially.

And one finding that should be taken more seriously than it has been: in the only adequately
powered non-European population available — 5,532 East Asian small-vessel stroke cases — the
storage signal **does not replicate**. That cannot be waved away as European genetic
instruments failing to transfer, because in the very same analysis, instruments for visceral
fat and for BMI transferred fine. Either the architecture genuinely differs across
populations, or the European result is inflated. That question is open and it is the largest
outstanding threat to everything above.

## On being scooped, and what is actually ours

While this was going on, another group published the headline. Myserlis and colleagues, in
*Neurology* in 2025, ran the same gluteofemoral fat instruments against the same stroke
subtypes and reached the same conclusion we did about mediation — that ordinary vascular
risk factors carry most of the effect.

That is worth stating plainly rather than burying. Our cerebral result is a **replication**,
not a discovery. Arriving second at the same answer by an independent route is genuinely
useful information about whether the answer is right, and it is not a contribution to the
literature.

What that paper did not do — and what remains unclaimed — is the kidney. It used coronary
disease and carotid thickening as positive controls and no renal endpoint at all. And
nobody, as far as we can find, has used a post-challenge insulin-sensitivity measure as a
genetic instrument for anything, because the necessary dataset only recently existed.

## The honest bottom line

Diabetes damages the brain's small vessels. It does so substantially through blood pressure
rather than through blood sugar, and the "safe fat storage" idea — attractive, mechanistic,
and genuinely supported in its molecular details — explains the **kidney** better than it
explains the brain.

The single largest obstacle is not analytical. It is that **the necessary dataset does not
exist anywhere in the world**. To ask whether something raises stroke risk *specifically
among diabetics*, you need a study where the comparison group also has diabetes. We
enumerated every endpoint definition in FinnGen across three data releases — roughly five
thousand — and every study in the GWAS Catalog. Exactly twelve require the control group to
have diabetes. All twelve are eye, kidney, or nerve. **None is the brain.** The largest
published study of brain white matter within diabetes anywhere is 465 people.

Creating that endpoint is not a new experiment. It is a definition-file change in a biobank
that already runs precisely this machinery for diabetic retinopathy and diabetic kidney
disease. It needs no new participants and no new consent. It is the cheapest high-value
thing anyone could do for this question, and nobody has asked for it.

That is the ask this work ends on.
