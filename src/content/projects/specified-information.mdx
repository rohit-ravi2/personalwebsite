## The puzzle

*C. elegans* is the only animal whose entire nervous system has been mapped, connection
by connection. Its wiring is famously **reproducible** — build a fresh worm and you get
essentially the same diagram, roughly the same ~7,000 chemical synapses in roughly the
same places, every time.

That reproducibility is the puzzle. The worm's genome is tiny. It cannot possibly store a
literal list of "neuron A connects to neuron B" for every connection — there simply isn't
room. So the wiring must be **generated** from something much more compact: a rulebook, a
molecular address system, a developmental program. Somewhere there is *specified
information* that the genome executes to build the same brain twice.

The obvious question — and the one this project chased — is **where that information
actually lives.** Is there a hidden "wiring code" waiting to be found? Or is the wiring a
by-product of simpler things, with no code at all?

This page is the honest log of the hunt. It reads as a sequence of hypotheses, each one
tested with pre-registered pass/fail gates and proper statistical controls, and each one
either kept or ruled out — with the reason it failed, and how that failure pointed to the
next thing to try.

## The honest bottom line

At the resolution I could test (adult neurons, class-level gene expression, a handful of
animals), the reproducible worm connectome does **not** show a distributed molecular
blueprint. Wherever an aggregate hidden code could have lived — gene-similarity matching,
a learned pairing grammar, a gene-regulatory generator, a lineage rulebook, a bioelectric
field — a proper control dissolved it into one of three plain ingredients:

- **physical contact** (who touches whom, and how much),
- **developmental timing** (when each neuron is born — you cannot wire a cell before it
  exists), and
- a **small sender-side transmitter bias.**

Together these explain most of the reliable "strong core" of the wiring. An earlier
version of this page said they explain it right up to the animal-to-animal noise ceiling.
That was wrong: I compared against a two-adult agreement score that is only a lower bound
on the ceiling, a richer model went past it, and on neurons held out entirely the contact
model drops to an AUC of 0.746. What is left over is not noise either. The per-connection
residual is reproducible (split-half reliability 0.855 across four L1 animals) and
structured, and most of the per-neuron variation in how many connections a cell makes is
still unexplained.

The nulls are also narrower than they look. Every molecular test used mature mRNA at the
level of neuron classes, so microRNAs and other small RNAs were never measured, left and
right cells could not be told apart, and there is no surface-protein data from the time
wiring happens. Inside those limits, some specific rules do have codes: innexin expression
predicts gap junctions, homeobox genes predict effector identity, and one guidance pair,
Slit–Robo (SLT-1 to SAX-3), marks reproducible synapses that form against the grain of the
geometry, replicated on a held-out connectome.

Put simply: the genome seems to specify a **physical-and-temporal scaffold** plus a sparse
set of rule-specific codes, not an edge-by-edge wiring table, at least at the resolution
available.

A note on honesty: this is a rigorous <em>decomposition</em>, not a discovered blueprint.
That contact dominates wiring echoes Brittin et al. (2021); that the wiring is reproducible
echoes Witvliet et al. (2021). The contribution here is the developmental-timing
mediation, the controlled transmitter bias, the systematic testing of hidden-code hypotheses
against matched nulls, and the record of which of my own conclusions did not survive.
