## The problem

The NeurIPS 2025 Open Polymer Prediction competition on Kaggle gives you a polymer as a SMILES
string for its repeat unit, with `*` marking where the chain continues, and asks for five
properties: glass transition temperature (Tg), fractional free volume (FFV), thermal
conductivity (Tc), density, and radius of gyration (Rg).

The training file has 7,973 polymers, but the labels are very sparse. FFV is measured for 7,030
of them. Tg has 511 labels, Tc 737, density 613 and Rg 614. So four of the five targets are
small-data regression problems sitting inside one larger table.

The score is a weighted MAE across the five targets. As implemented in my notebooks
(`competition_wmae` in `phase1.ipynb`), each target gets weight 1 / (√n × range), where n is its
label count and range is the spread of its labelled values, and the weights are normalised to sum
to one. That makes the weighting very uneven: Tc gets 0.561, density 0.269, FFV 0.158, Rg 0.012
and Tg 0.001. Tg, the property a polymer chemist would care about most, barely moves the score,
because its range is 620 °C.

## Local results

All numbers below are out-of-fold (OOF) MAE from `phase4.ipynb`, recomputed from the saved
predictions (`oof_lgb.csv`, `oof_cat.csv`) against the competition training labels. The protocol is
5-fold GroupKFold grouped on canonical SMILES, so a duplicate polymer never sits in both the
training and validation side. Each target is trained only on its own labelled rows, and the OOF
predictions are averaged over 3 seeds. Every labelled row has exactly one OOF prediction.

The baseline predicts, for each validation fold, the mean of that target over the training folds.
Recomputing it with a plain shuffled KFold gives the same number to three decimals (0.168).

| Model | Weighted MAE | Tg (°C) | FFV | Tc | Density (g/cm³) | Rg (Å) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Predict the fold mean | 0.1685 | 88.4 | 0.0209 | 0.0770 | 0.1096 | 3.96 |
| CatBoost | 0.0674 | 51.1 | 0.00572 | 0.0252 | 0.0274 | 1.57 |
| LightGBM | 0.0649 | 48.6 | 0.00576 | 0.0252 | 0.0247 | 1.53 |
| 50/50 blend (not tuned) | **0.0647** | 49.0 | 0.00552 | 0.0249 | 0.0245 | 1.52 |

![Weighted out-of-fold MAE for the fold-mean baseline, CatBoost, LightGBM and a 50/50 blend, and per-target error relative to the baseline](/images/projects/neurips-polymer-prediction-results.png)

LightGBM cuts the baseline's weighted error by 61%. Per target, its error is 22% of the baseline
for density, 27% for FFV, 33% for Tc, 39% for Rg and 55% for Tg. The notebook includes an
NNLS blend over OOF predictions, but that cell was never run, so no fitted blend weights exist.
The 50/50 row is a plain average, and its 0.0002 gain over LightGBM alone is within what I would
expect from noise.

Two things make these numbers somewhat optimistic. Both models early-stop on the same fold they
are scored on, and the gain-pruning step that picks the 938 features was fit on all labelled rows
before the CV split. Neither changes the gap to the baseline, but the absolute values would rise a
little under nested validation. I made a Kaggle submission, but I am reporting only these local
numbers.

## Where the signal came from

**Whole-molecule descriptors, not substructure bits.** The phase4 notebook doesn't save its models,
so for feature importance I used the five-fold LightGBM models saved in the repo's first commit.
They use the phase1 recipe: 1,412 Mordred 2D descriptors, 2,048 Morgan radius-2 count bits and
8 RDKit basics. Summing split gain over the five folds, Mordred descriptors carry 98.5% of the
gain for Tg, 96.2% for FFV, 91.5% for Tc, 99.6% for density and 97.8% for Rg. That is despite the
Morgan bits outnumbering them. The pruning step in phase4 says the same thing: it kept 75% of the
Mordred columns but only 13% of the Morgan radius-2 counts and 36% of the radius-3 bits.

The top features per target are mostly physically sensible:

- **Tg:** `RotRatio`, the fraction of rotatable bonds, is the single largest feature (5.0% of
  gain). Chain flexibility is the textbook driver of the glass transition.
- **Density:** mean atomic mass (`Mm`), mean atomic number (`MZ`) and average molecular weight
  (`AMW`), plus information-content indices. Heavier atoms per unit of structure mean a denser
  material.
- **Rg:** one descriptor, `MDEC-11` (a distance-edge count between primary carbons, effectively a
  measure of how many chain ends there are and how far apart they sit), carries 13% of the gain on
  its own.
- **Tc:** the one target where a fingerprint bit leads: Morgan count bit 1143 takes 6.1% of the
  gain, ahead of topological charge indices (`JGI2`, `JGT10`).
- **FFV:** gain is spread thinly. The top feature has 1.0%, and the top 20 together have only 10%.
  This is the target with 7,030 labels, and the model uses many weak descriptors rather than a few
  strong ones.

**LightGBM against CatBoost.** On the same pruned features, the two models' OOF predictions
correlate at 0.97 to 0.99 per target. LightGBM has the lower error on a given row only about half
the time (49 to 53%). That is why averaging them helps so little.

## Why this architecture

The label counts settled most of it. With 511 to 737 labels on four targets, a model that has to
learn chemistry from raw structure (a graph network or a fine-tuned SMILES transformer) has too
little data. Descriptor libraries like Mordred already encode chemistry: mass, polarity, shape and
flexibility. Gradient-boosted trees handle that kind of wide, heterogeneous, partly redundant
table well, without scaling, and they train in minutes per target. Every model is per-target and
trained on masked labels, so FFV's 7,030 rows never swamp the small targets.

The sequence of notebooks:

- **`phase1.ipynb`**: the first end-to-end baseline (Mordred, Morgan counts and RDKit basics,
  2,444 features, one LightGBM per target). Its own output reports a weighted MAE of 0.06827, but
  it used plain shuffled folds, so duplicate polymers could leak across a split.
- **`phase2.ipynb`**: label-sparsity audit, a small physics-motivated feature block, a
  predict-the-mean check, and external Tg tables collected. No external data entered a scored model.
- **`phase4.ipynb`**: the reference pipeline above. It adds MACCS keys and radius-3 Morgan bits,
  variance and correlation filters (2,641 features), gain pruning (938), grouped folds, CatBoost
  as a second model family, and 3 seeds. A Tanimoto-kernel SVR and the NNLS blend are written in
  the notebook but were not executed.
- **`phase5.ipynb` and `hybridpipeline.ipynb`**: the hybrid attempt. It adds 768-dimensional
  ChemBERTa embeddings, Murcko-scaffold folds and a GINE graph network. **This did not produce a
  usable comparison, so I make no claim that it improved anything.** The scored phase5 LightGBM
  run set `min_data_in_leaf = 200` on folds with only 316 to 489 training rows. In 2 of 4 Tg folds
  and 1 of 2 Tc folds, LightGBM found no usable features and predicted a constant. Its Tg MAE of
  92.1 is worse than the fold-mean baseline. The GNN and stacking cells produced no scores, and
  `hybridpipeline.ipynb` was never run. So the question of whether learned embeddings help is
  still open.
- **`track3.ipynb`** is excluded: it merges a "physics" block that is literally the five target
  columns from `train.csv` into its features, so its scores measure leakage, not prediction.

The Kaggle-side notebook (`neurips-preparedataset-predict.ipynb`) runs inference with per-target
AutoGluon predictors trained outside this repo. I don't report a local score for them, because the
repo contains no OOF predictions from those models.

## Limits

These are 5-fold OOF scores on one set of grouped folds, with early stopping and feature pruning
that both saw the validation labels, so they are mildly optimistic. Grouping by exact canonical
SMILES is weaker than grouping by scaffold, and the hidden test set may be further from the
training data than these folds are. The feature importances come from the first-commit models
(phase1 recipe), not from the phase4 models, which were not saved. The embedding and GNN branch
is untested rather than negative. The clean test is to rerun phase5 with a leaf size scaled to each
target and the phase4 folds, so that the embedding columns are the only change.
