## The puzzle

*C. elegans* has the best-mapped nervous system of any animal: about 300 neurons and a
wiring diagram that has been redrawn from electron microscopy several times. It now also has
public whole-brain calcium recordings, with most neurons identified by name, in dozens of
freely moving animals.

So the obvious question is whether the wiring diagram helps predict the activity. If neuron A
synapses onto neuron B, does knowing A's recent activity help forecast B's, more than knowing
the activity of some other neuron that is not connected to B?

It is harder to answer than it looks. Connected neurons tend to sit close together in the
head, so any benefit from "connected" can be a benefit from "nearby". Nearby neurons can also
contaminate each other's signal under the microscope. And a model with more inputs will often
look better whether or not those inputs are the right ones. A fair test needs the same model
fitted with the real connectome, with shuffled copies that keep every neuron's number of
connections, and with a graph built from physical distance alone, all scored on animals the
model never saw.

## What survived

One graph neural network, trained five times with nothing changed except its edges. The real
connectome lands among its shuffled copies, nearest physical neighbours do slightly better, and
the edge gates it learns are no more consistent than the gates learned on shuffled graphs.

## Findings and limits

**What holds.** On Atanas et al. (2023), 38 animals forecast 2.4 s ahead with every animal
held out once, the identity of the wiring adds nothing measurable to a learned graph model:
connectome minus shuffled is +0.0001 R² [−0.0007, +0.0009], so the two do about as well as each
other. A graph of each neuron's nearest physical neighbours beats the connectome by 0.0023
[0.0007, 0.0036]. The linear models agree: the connectome beats its shuffled copies by a small,
significant margin (+0.0015 to +0.0023), but neighbours match or beat it at every horizon. In
the stimulation data, distance predicts the functional map far better than connectome weight
(AUC 0.727 vs 0.566), and the connectome adds nothing once distance is known. Having *some*
sparse graph helps a little (+0.0034 over no edges), nonlinearity helps more (+0.015), and
connecting everything to everything is destructive.

**What it does not show.** This is not evidence that the wiring is irrelevant to the worm.
It says that, at this resolution, these recordings cannot tell the wiring apart from geometry.
Several limits matter:

- **Nearness is confounded with ganglion and cell class.** Physically close neurons tend to share
  a ganglion and often a class (left/right partners sit together). The neighbour graph may be
  winning because it groups neurons that do similar things, not because of anything about
  distance itself. I did not build a class- or ganglion-matched null.
- **Only about 89 neurons are identified in enough animals**, sampled every 0.6 s through a
  calcium indicator. Fast or sparse synaptic effects could be invisible at this sampling.
- **The perturbation test is uninformative.** The Kato AVA silencing benchmark passes its
  manipulation check, but 5 animals per arm cannot separate any graph from any other.
- **The proximity effect in the stimulation data is not explained.** Its mechanism, optical
  contamination or local coupling, is non-identifiable from existing data. Two of my own
  numbers on it did not survive held-out checks: the contamination kernel explains 7–13% of the
  effect on held-out animals, not the 30% I first reported from a non-random discovery set, and a
  connectome lead of +0.03 AUC was mostly reproduced by a matched random control. An earlier claim
  that brain activity predicts future speed came from a look-ahead in the lag search and is
  retracted.

**Status: paused (October 2026).** The test that could change this result has not been run: a
benchmark on the Randi stimulation data that scores predictions with a model of measurement
contamination and compares the connectome against distance, geometry, the Creamer et al. linear
dynamical system, and matched-removal baselines. Two smaller items are queued behind it:
forecasting the oxygen-shift response in Kato's stimulus arm, and imputing held-out neurons.
Separating contamination from coupling needs new data rather than more analysis: fast two-plane
or voltage imaging with sparse indicator expression, or innexin degradation in the target cell.

<p style={{fontSize: "0.8rem", opacity: 0.7, marginTop: "1.5rem"}}>
Scope: this is a benchmark and a negative result on public data, not a new model of worm
dynamics. The data are Atanas et al. (2023), Kato et al. (2015) and Randi et al. (2023); the
connectome is White et al. (1986) and Witvliet et al. (2021) as shipped by the
<code>wormneuroatlas</code> package. Creamer, Leifer and Pillow (2024) fit connectome-constrained
dynamics to the Randi atlas; Randi et al. themselves report that signal propagation often
departs from what the anatomy predicts. What I add is the degree-preserving and physical-neighbour
controls applied to the same frozen, held-out evaluation, a check of the proximity effect against
an optical contamination model, and the record of which of my own claims did not hold up.
</p>
