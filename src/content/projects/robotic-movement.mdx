<Aside href="/posts/robotic-movement/origin-tennis/" label="Read how it started →">
**Origin.** This started with the tennis serve. I played and coached in college, and the thing I never stopped noticing is that a skinny kid with real technique out-serves a big, strong player who only uses his arm. Technique, not strength, decides the result, and for a robot, unlike a person, the optimal technique is computable from its own specification.
</Aside>

## How these movements were made: no demonstrations, no learned policy

<Aside href="/posts/robotic-movement/no-demonstrations/">
The pipeline searches for movement using a robot model and a physical objective, without human demonstrations. Hand-designed starting structures and balance controllers are described below.
</Aside>

Nothing on this page was learned from people. No motion capture, no teleoperation, no video of
humans, no imitation dataset, and no neural network anywhere in the pipeline. Each movement is
**constructed**:

1. **The robot's own specification** goes in: its geometry, masses, joint limits and each motor's
   rated torque and speed, read from the manufacturer's model.
2. **A physical goal** goes in: release a ball fast, move the body forward, keep a load on the head.
   The goal says *what* must happen, never *how*.
3. **A starting motion is derived from the body**: which joint motions move the hand or the body
   most effectively given each motor's limits.
4. **Search on real physics** refines it, and the robot discovers the technique by trial in simulation,
   checked against the motors' real limits at two simulation time steps.

The only "experience" is simulation the system generates for itself, roughly 2,400 to 38,400 trial
runs per technique (minutes to a few hours on a desktop CPU). That is the whole cost of a new skill
on a new body.

| | Typical learned-robot approach | This work |
|---|---|---|
| Human demonstrations | thousands of examples per skill | **none** |
| Learned model | large neural policy | **none** |
| New robot | recollect data, retrain | **same code, new spec file** |
| What the robot needs | a dataset | **its own specification and a goal** |

The one place human data appears is the first jump clip below, a recorded human jump replayed on
the robot as a comparison baseline. The motion the robot derived from its own body beat it.

What *is* supplied by hand, and stated with every result: the broad structure of some starting
motions (for example "wind up, then release"), the balance and foot-placement controllers, and the
simulator settings. The movements themselves are found, not authored.

## Jumping

<Aside href="/posts/robotic-movement/jumping/">
I expected the robot's best jump to look nothing like a person's. Instead it found the human squat jump, exaggerated: a crouch far deeper than any of us can use, and almost twice the takeoff speed of a recorded human jump replayed on the same robot.
</Aside>

## Throwing

<Aside href="/posts/robotic-movement/the-throw/">
This is the animation that changed the project. The arm lagged back, the hips swivelled further than any person's can, and the result was a baseball throw pushed beyond human range by the robot's own form. Nobody told it to throw like a pitcher, or to go past one.
</Aside>

<Clip src="/videos/robotic-movement/throw-3-robot-derived-windup.mp4" caption="A wind-up derived from the robot's own body, refined by search: hips swivel on planted feet, the arm trails the trunk." note="Historical render: this version exceeded the wrist's rated speed and is not a valid speed figure. The same construction under the specified actuator limits reaches 20-23 m/s." />

### Throwing at a target

The goal is no longer "as fast as possible" but "land the ball within 0.5 m of a target". The body is the stock G1, its motors are held to their published torque ratings with a 10 ms response lag, and the ball is free once released.

<Pair>
<Clip src="/videos/robotic-movement/throw-target-10m.mp4" caption="10 m target: the ball lands 0.12 m from the centre (9.4 m/s release). It still hits on 14 of 16 randomized versions of the robot and its start that the search never saw." note="The hand holds the ball by an attachment until release, not a finger grip. Ball flight is drag-free. One smaller independent check passed only 2 of 4, so the margin is thin." />
<Clip src="/videos/robotic-movement/throw-target-15m.mp4" caption="15 m target: hit on the nominal robot (0.42 m from the centre, 11.5 m/s), but not reliably. ## Kicking

<Aside href="/posts/robotic-movement/the-throw/">
Given only "send the ball fast from one foot", the kick came out strictly sequenced, pelvis to thigh to shank to foot: the same chain as a tennis serve, found from scratch in a different limb.
</Aside>

<Clip src="/videos/robotic-movement/kick-ball.mp4" caption="G1 kicks a simulated football from one-foot support: 11.8 m/s ball speed. The weight shift, foot lift and hip-to-foot sequencing came out of the construction, not a script." note="Earlier, looser checks: this kick was not tested against the joint-stop and after-the-kick balance checks used below." />

<Clip src="/videos/robotic-movement/kick-goal-5m.mp4" caption="Aimed kick into a 2 m goal 5 m away, on the stock G1 with rated torques and a 10 ms motor lag: 4.9 m/s, scored on all 16 randomized versions it was tested on and on 4 more from an independent check." note="Honest limits: the robot falls after every kick. Only 1 of the 12 goal setups tried passes this reliably, and the angled goals found nothing valid. The ankles hitting their joint stops are what limits it. A small sphere on the foot (an attachment) makes the ball contact." />

## Running

<Aside href="/posts/robotic-movement/running/">
I wanted one test of pure physics efficiency, and max sprint speed is it. The surprise was the wall: at stock ratings the ankles are too weak to push off through the foot at all, and Apollo, a robot never used to build the method, ran with no code change.
</Aside>

## Walking

<Aside href="/posts/robotic-movement/running/">
Walking is the case people assume is solved. The bar I care about is a robot that essentially never tips over, and this section shows both how close that is and where it still fails.
</Aside>

Walking is the dull-looking hard case: a periodic gait with both feet sharing the load at every step, no sliding, steady speed and heading. Both robots use their stock bodies and published motor ratings with a 10 ms motor lag, and each walk was checked at two physics time steps.

<Pair>

## Walking with a load on the head

<Aside href="/posts/robotic-movement/carrying/">
People do not carry a pot on their head with a ring. They balance it with their arms, and that is the nuance I wanted the robot to learn.
</Aside>

The body-derived arm-balance law from the standing head carry, now while walking at 0.75 m/s. The load is a free object resting on the head, not attached.

## Carrying with the body's structure

<Aside href="/posts/robotic-movement/carrying/">
Leverage is something people understand and robots do not yet. With the arms balancing a load on its head, the robot carries about five times what its hands can, and given a bag too heavy to lift, it leaned the bag against its own hips, which nobody specified.
</Aside>

<Clip src="/videos/robotic-movement/headcarry-box-32kg-lag.mp4" caption="G1 with hands, standing: a free 32 kg box resting on its head, kept there by the arms, with a 10 ms motor lag. The balancing law is built from the arm's own geometry and motor limits. Second half: the same posture with the law switched off, and the box falls in under a second. Hands-only carrying tops out at 6-8 kg." note="Requires a cap on the servo gains (a controller choice). Without motor lag, a 40 kg pot also holds. With lag, 40 kg is fragile." />
<Clip src="/videos/robotic-movement/luggage-tip-and-roll.mp4" caption="A 45 kg bag is too heavy to lift; the search tips it onto its wheels and leans it against the hips instead, a use of the body nobody specified." note="Hands attached to the handle (not a finger grip)." />
