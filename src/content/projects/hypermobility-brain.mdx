## Where this started

The question came from a practicing physician in the family, who had noticed something
across years of seeing patients: the people who could bend their thumbs back to their
wrists, who described themselves as double-jointed, who dislocated shoulders reaching for
things on high shelves — those same patients seemed unusually likely to also have
migraines, attention problems, anxiety, chronic pain, and a stubborn constellation of
symptoms that no single specialist owned.

That is a clinical hunch, and clinical hunches are usually wrong. This one is not.

## What hypermobility is

Some people's joints move further than most people's do. It is measured with a nine-point
checklist — can you bend your little finger back past ninety degrees, can you touch your
thumb to your forearm, do your elbows and knees hyperextend, can you put your palms flat
on the floor with straight legs. Score high enough and you are "hypermobile."

At the mild end this is just a way bodies vary, and it is common. At the more severe end
it becomes hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, where the loose joints come with chronic
pain, frequent dislocations, fatigue, digestive problems, and dizziness on standing.

The tissue involved is connective tissue — collagen and the surrounding scaffolding that
holds the body together. So a joint problem. Structural. Mechanical. Nothing obviously to
do with the brain.

## The correlation is real

This is the part that keeps being confirmed, and it is genuinely strange.

**Migraine.** In a study of more than 1.6 million young adults screened at military
conscription — a setting where nobody self-selects into a clinic, and where most of the
participants were male — hypermobile people had roughly **double** the odds of migraine.
That design matters, because most hypermobility research happens in specialist clinics
full of people who sought out a diagnosis, which is a recipe for spurious associations.
Conscription screening dodges that entirely, and the effect survives.

**ADHD.** Swedish national health registers, covering nearly twelve thousand people with
connective tissue diagnoses, found ADHD at about **five and a half times** the expected
rate. It replicated across two separate diagnostic codes, and it held up when the same
group physically examined joints in a psychiatric sample rather than relying on medical
records.

**Fibromyalgia.** A 2026 study across two and a half million people measured the
*genetic* correlation between fibromyalgia and hypermobility at **0.79** — on a scale
where 1.0 would mean identical genetic influences. That is not a mild statistical
relationship. That is two conditions sharing most of their genetic architecture.

So: a connective tissue difference in the limbs travels with headaches, attention, pain
and mood. Nobody disputes the association anymore. The open question is *why*.

## The beautiful explanation, and why we tested it

There is an idea in this space that is almost too elegant to resist.

The brain has its own connective tissue. Certain neurons are wrapped in structures called
perineuronal nets — a mesh of the same broad family of molecules that makes up
cartilage and ligament. These nets are the brain's brake on plasticity. During
development, while the nets are still thin, circuits rewire easily. As the nets thicken
and lock into place, the window closes and learning gets harder. Dissolve them
experimentally in an adult animal and you can reopen a childhood-like window.

So the hypothesis writes itself. If a person's scaffolding molecules are built a little
differently, you would get looser ligaments **and** thinner brain nets from the same
underlying cause. A bendy body and a bendy mind, as siblings rather than as cause and
effect. It would explain the whole cluster in one stroke.

We built the pipeline to test it properly. Using published genetic data from hundreds of
thousands of people, we asked a specific question: do the genes that build connective
tissue carry more of the inherited risk for ADHD, autism, anxiety and depression than
ordinary genes do?

## It failed, cleanly

Nothing survived. Not close.

The cleanest way to say it: in depression, about 2.33% of all human genes reach
statistical significance. Among connective tissue genes, the figure was **2.26%**. They
were being hit at exactly the background rate, which is what "no relationship" looks like.

We had also built in a control — sets of random genes matched for length, because
connective tissue genes are unusually long and long genes produce false positives. One of
the random control sets scored *better* than our real candidate set. Whatever signal we
thought we saw was indistinguishable from noise.

Two independent findings support that null. A separate group ran a comparable analysis on
depression in eight hundred thousand people and got the same nothing. And, more
decisively, someone had already measured the thing the theory predicts: researchers put
people with Ehlers-Danlos syndrome through motor learning tasks, four separate times
across four different experimental setups, and their brain plasticity came back
**completely normal** every time.

Then a look at the underlying biology finished it off. Perineuronal nets turn out to
contain no collagen at all. They are built by neurons themselves, out of a different
molecular family, in a part of the brain physically separate from where the
connective tissue genes are active. The two gene sets do not overlap and are not
co-regulated. The elegant explanation was not underpowered. It was anatomically wrong.

It did not help that two of the molecules involved share a family name — tenascin-X causes
a form of Ehlers-Danlos, tenascin-R sits in perineuronal nets — while being unrelated
genes on different chromosomes. That coincidence made the story look more connected than
it was.

## What we did find

Along the way the analysis produced results of its own.

We estimated, apparently for the first time, how heritable clinically diagnosed
hypermobility is from common genetic variants: about **9%**. Modest, but real and
statistically solid.

We then measured how much genetic architecture hypermobility shares with the surgical
conditions that loose connective tissue causes — hernias, varicose veins, pelvic organ
prolapse. All three came back positive, in the range of **0.24 to 0.28**.

The interesting part was the internal structure. Those three conditions are only weakly
related to *each other* — varicose veins and prolapse share almost nothing. Yet
hypermobility sits equally close to all three, nearer to the centre of that space than
any of them are to one another. Hypermobility appears to be a better single readout of
general connective tissue looseness than any of the conditions doctors actually operate on.

We also found a specific gene worth watching. A variant in PIEZO1 — which encodes a
protein that lets cells physically sense being stretched — had been reported as associated
with joint flexibility in a study that was never replicated. Tested against an independent
Finnish population, it came back positive, modestly but in the right direction. The same
genetic location is overwhelmingly associated with varicose veins. It looks like a genuine
hub for tissue looseness.

## Things that turned out not to be true

A large part of the value here was subtraction. Along the way, several widely repeated
claims did not survive checking:

- The often-quoted figure that hypermobile people have **seven times** the rate of autism
  comes from the wrong patient group. In the population that actually corresponds to
  hypermobility, the figure is about **1.4 times** — and a community sample of schoolchildren
  found no relationship at all.
- The idea that hypermobile people have poor proprioception — a worse sense of where their
  limbs are — is contested rather than established. Two systematic reviews disagree, the
  larger body of evidence finds nothing, and three studies found hypermobile people
  performed *better*. The deficit tracks having symptoms, not having flexible joints.
- The claimed link to floppy heart valves is largely an artefact of 1980s imaging. When
  patients were re-scanned with modern equipment, five of seven prior diagnoses disappeared.
- Loose joints do not wear out early. Across more than eight thousand people in community
  studies, hypermobility is associated with **less** osteoarthritis, not more.

## The thread still worth pulling

Two things came out of this that point somewhere.

The first is a reframe. Every study that measured plasticity in hypermobile people
measured how *far* they adapted, and found it normal. Nobody has measured how *fast*.
That distinction matters, because there is a principled reason to expect speed to differ.
A hypermobile limb is genuinely less predictable — the joint has more play, and the same
muscle command produces a more variable result. In any system that learns from experience,
operating in a more variable world rationally calls for updating faster. If that is right,
hypermobile people should learn motor tasks *more quickly* and forget them sooner. Nobody
has ever run that experiment. The prediction is the opposite of the deficit framing the
field has assumed for forty years.

The second is chemistry. The forms of Ehlers-Danlos caused by problems making sugar chains
— rather than problems making collagen fibres — are the ones whose genes are actually
active in the brain. Independently, the single strongest genetic signal in a study of
119,000 people with back pain turned out to be an enzyme that attaches sulfate to those
same sugar chains. And there is one gene, aggrecan, whose partial loss causes joint
laxity *and* which is required for perineuronal nets to work — one gene, both phenotypes,
each published separately and never connected.

That is not a mechanism yet. It is a coincidence that keeps happening in the same place.

## The honest bottom line

The correlation between flexible joints and this cluster of neurological and psychiatric
conditions is real, replicated in populations that avoid the usual biases, and partly
genetic. We can measure it.

We cannot explain it. The most attractive molecular explanation is now ruled out, and
ruled out for a specific and instructive reason rather than for lack of data. What
connects a loose ligament to a migraine remains unknown.

Settling it needs data that does not exist yet — a study measuring learning speed rather
than learning extent, and access to cohorts that hold both joint measurements and
molecular data on the same people. Both are identified. Neither has been done. This page
will be updated when they are.
