## The puzzle

The question grew out of my interest in how neural function can be disrupted by processes a
structural scan does not show.

Myelin, the insulating sheath that lets axons conduct fast, is mostly lipid. Building it means
moving a large amount of cholesterol and fatty acid into membrane on a developmental schedule,
and the prefrontal cortex is the last region to finish: it is still myelinating years after the
rest of the brain. So the starting hypothesis was structural. If someone inherits a weak
lipid-supply chain (synthesis, transport, uptake), the place that should fail first is the one
that needs the most supply latest, and that is the region whose functions look most like the
ones affected in autism. Weak supply → oligodendrocytes that cannot keep up → thin or late
frontal myelin → mistimed signalling.

That is a hypothesis, not a known fact, and it is testable because every arrow in it leaves a
mark in public data. Inherited risk shows up in GWAS and de novo mutation data; cell-type
localisation shows up in single-cell atlases; perturbation shows up in methylation; and
timing can be computed.

Two rules were set before anything ran. Do not route the question through APOE4, the obvious
lipid gene. And agree on a plausible path before running tests, rather than running everything
and reading the winners. The standard for the result was that it be novel, meaningful and useful,
all three at once.

## Findings and limits

What holds, at the resolution I could test:

- **A weak, global white-matter correlation.** Autism liability is genetically correlated with
  lower neurite density in all 15 tracts tested (mean rg −0.102, best FDR 0.064), with no
  frontal gradient. It replicates Gu et al. (2025) with less power. The frontal part of the
  original hypothesis is gone; the direction is not.
- **Chromatin, for rare variants.** De novo loss-of-function burden in chromatin regulators
  (OR 2.41, clean synonymous and missense controls) is the established autism result, and it is
  not oligodendrocyte-specific. The same module is null for common variants.
- **A modest oligodendrocyte-gene enrichment among autism genes** (1.39× after annotation-count
  matching, 2.38× in the highest-confidence tier), secondary to neuronal and synaptic signal and
  expressed mostly after birth. The β-catenin/TCF complex is the one leave-one-out-robust gene
  set, but its genes are not oligodendrocyte-enriched.
- **A modelling result.** Under- and over-myelination move conduction spread in opposite
  directions, so a cohort mixing both cancels. Whole-cohort myelin studies of autism should be
  expected to come back weak, and stratifying by gene or trajectory is a precondition.

What died: lipid and cholesterol genes as an entry point (every common-variant, rare-variant
and gene-set instrument, plus methylation at the power available), APOE, frontal specificity,
localisation of common-variant risk to an oligodendrocyte maturation stage (two atlases), the
neurogenic-to-gliogenic switch, apoptosis and pruning genes as loci of risk, a direct
cytokine route to OPCs, an inherited child-genome immune route, and PV-interneuron × OPC
co-dysregulation in autistic cortex.

The most meaningful signal is in OPC methylation. Oligodendrocyte precursors carry the highest
rate of diagnosis-associated methylation in autistic prefrontal cortex, and the excess is not
noise: matched on measurement precision it grows from OR 1.92 to 2.86 (95% CI 2.50–3.29,
p = 4e-56), and it strengthens as the significance threshold tightens. What is still open is how
specific it is to OPCs, because each cell type was tested on its own set of regions; a shared
region set refit from the per-donor files will settle that. A second lead is open rather than
claimed: the maturation clock shows an
age-by-diagnosis interaction in oligodendrocytes, which is the shape the timing idea predicts,
but it was post hoc and one of its controls was not clean.

The nulls are narrower than they look. The common-variant tests rest on one autism GWAS of modest size and
adult UK Biobank imaging; the single-cell and methylation data are postnatal and postmortem;
the autistic-cortex comparisons have 15 to 16 donors per group; and diffusion measures track
axons and neurites, not myelin itself. Some planned checks never ran: a replication of the
gene-set tests on a second autism GWAS (it failed silently), a chromatin S-LDSC annotation, the
Mendelian randomisation after the allele fix, and the allergy–autism overlap tests.

The tests that would settle the timing question need data I do not yet have access to:

- **HBN-POD2** diffusion data are downloaded, but diagnosis labels need the Child Mind
  Institute data-use agreement. With them: is lower axonal water fraction specific to autistic
  children, and is it frontal or global?
- **IBIS** infant imaging at 6, 12 and 24 months needs NIMH Data Archive access. Infants later
  diagnosed with autism show higher white-matter anisotropy at 6 months and lower at 24
  (Wolff et al. 2012). A trajectory account predicts a difference in the rate of change, which
  only longitudinal infant data can test.
- **Per-donor methylation files** for GSE298243 would settle whether the OPC excess holds on a
  shared region set with diagnosis permuted across donors.
- An experiment for collaborators: restore neuronal activity in an animal model with an
  established oligodendrocyte phenotype (for example NLGN3) and see whether the myelin change
  reverses. That would separate cause from consequence, and as far as I can find it has not
  been done.
